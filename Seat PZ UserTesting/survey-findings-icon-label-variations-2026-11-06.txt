SURVEY FINDINGS: ICON AND LABEL VARIATIONS FOR PREMIUM ZONE LABEL ON TRIP CARD
4 UserTesting surveys, unmoderated
Goal: assess whether the icon (Crown vs Diamond) or the label ("Zone" vs "Experience") — or a combination — changes overall perception and expectations of the premium offering


VARIATIONS TESTED
- V1: Crown icon + "Premium Zone"
- V2: Diamond icon + "Premium Zone"
- V3: Crown icon + "Premium Experience"
- V4: Diamond icon + "Premium Experience"


KEY FINDINGS

1. The label matters more than the icon
   The switch from "Zone" to "Experience" had a stronger effect on perception and expectations
   than the switch from Crown to Diamond. Participants who saw "Experience" associated
   it more broadly with a lifestyle or service quality, while "Zone" anchored expectations
   to a physical area of the bus.

2. "Experience" scores slightly higher overall, but creates more confusion
   Average perceived value score: 3.75 ("Experience") vs 3.61 ("Zone").
   However, "Experience" triggered broader — and sometimes misaligned — expectations:
   some participants expected food, drink service, or lounge-style amenities.
   This raised the bar for delivery and increased the risk of disappointment.

3. "Zone" sets clearer, more grounded expectations
   Participants who saw "Zone" more reliably connected the label to a physical seat
   location (upper deck, front rows) and a defined set of seat-level benefits.
   Fewer participants over-interpreted what was included.

4. Icon had minimal standalone effect
   Crown vs Diamond did not produce meaningfully different expectations or scores
   within the same label group. The icon serves mainly as a visual differentiator
   on the trip card rather than a signal about what the premium offering includes.

5. Discoverability and understanding remain separate problems
   Regardless of variation, most participants did not proactively seek out extra info.
   Those who did find the PZ panel or seat categories panel responded positively.
   The bigger barrier is making the entry points more obvious, not the label or icon choice.


VARIATION BREAKDOWN

V1 — Crown + "Premium Zone"
- Perception: grounded, seat and location focused
- Common interpretation: better seats at the front/upper deck
- Confusion areas: some participants thought the premium zone price was included
  in the trip fare, not an add-on
- Notable: participants who found the seat categories panel became noticeably
  more interested and confident in their choice

V2 — Diamond + "Premium Zone"
- Perception: similar to V1; "Zone" framing dominant over icon change
- Notable: one participant explicitly linked it to a physical space ("so you do
  have to pay a premium") only after seeing the seat map — the label alone
  did not fully communicate it was an add-on cost

V3 — Crown + "Premium Experience"
- Perception: more experiential, some food/drink associations
- Higher score but with wider variance
- Notable: participants more likely to feel let down when they arrived at a
  seat selection screen rather than a service-level upgrade page

V4 — Diamond + "Premium Experience"
- Perception: similar to V3; broader experiential expectations
- Notable: the combination produced the highest initial interest but also
  the most misaligned mental models about what was actually included


RECOMMENDATIONS

Prefer "Zone" over "Experience" if the premium offering is seat-focused.
The label sets more accurate expectations and reduces disappointment risk.

If "Experience" is chosen, the information architecture needs to work harder:
- Make the PZ panel or seat categories panel more discoverable at the results stage
- Surface a brief summary (e.g. 2-3 bullet features) earlier in the flow,
  before the user reaches the seat map
- Manage expectations explicitly: clarify it is a seat upgrade, not a service upgrade

The icon can be chosen on visual/brand grounds — it does not carry enough
semantic weight to change user expectations on its own.
